The impact of AI

AI and war

This page covers war, including the deaths of civilians and children.

AI has become part of how wars are fought.

Armies use it to sort intelligence, choose targets and guide drones. Its appeal is speed and scale. A small team with AI can do work that once took thousands of people, and plan bigger, more coordinated attacks. But in war a wrong answer can cost lives, and the faster and bigger the system, the harder it is for anyone to catch a mistake.

What it looks like

What you need to know

  1. AI makes war faster, and leaves less time to check.

    A person is usually still meant to approve each strike. But AI can suggest far more targets than a team of people could check properly, and armies are under pressure to act fast. The check can shrink to a glance, so the person ends up agreeing to the machine's choice rather than making their own. In Gaza, Israeli intelligence officers said in 2024 that they spent about 20 seconds on each target an AI system suggested (+972 Magazine). The Israeli army says an analyst verifies every target.

  2. AI is only as good as what it's given.

    AI works from the information people feed it. If that information is old or wrong, the system can turn a mistake into a target quickly, and people may trust it more because a machine produced it. The faster the system, the faster an old mistake spreads.

  3. Some weapons don't wait for a person.

    Armies often jam the radio link between a drone and the person flying it. A drone with AI on board doesn't need that link. It can find and strike a target by itself, which gives armies a strong reason to build them (RFE/RL, 2024). With no link, no one can call it back.

  4. Armies and companies set the limits, mostly in private.

    There's no global treaty on weapons that act on their own. Each army sets its own rules, and each company decides what its AI can be used for. Those limits can move. Google dropped its pledge not to build AI for weapons in 2025 (TechCrunch). After Anthropic refused to let its AI be used for fully autonomous weapons, the Pentagon labelled it a risk to the military's supply chain in 2026 (TechCrunch). Most of what we know comes from leaks and investigations, not from armies or companies.

What's at stake

This could go either way. AI could help armies tell soldiers from civilians, and catch mistakes before they're made. Or people could hand it more of each decision, until a person's approval is only a formality. When a machine picks the target and a person has seconds to agree, whose decision is it really? Which way it goes depends on whether people keep real control, whether countries agree limits, and whether armies have to show the results.

How big is it?

In real use, where we can see it.

  • The US military's Maven system, run by Palantir under a $1.3 billion contract, combines sensor data, classifies targets and builds strike plans. The Washington Post reported that, working with Anthropic's Claude, it suggested hundreds of targets with their coordinates in the first 24 hours of the 2026 US-Iran war (Washington Post and Military Times, 2026). US officials say Maven helped identify and strike more than 2,000 targets in the first 96 hours (claimed by officials, Military Times, October 2026).
  • In a 2024 US Army exercise, about 20 soldiers using Maven did the targeting work that took more than 2,000 people in the 2003 invasion of Iraq (measured in an exercise, CSET, via Military Times, August 2024).
  • An Israeli military intelligence unit stored recordings of Palestinians' phone calls on Microsoft's cloud, the Guardian reported in 2025. Microsoft's own review supported parts of that reporting, and it cut off some of the unit's cloud storage. "We do not provide technology to facilitate mass surveillance of civilians," said Microsoft's president, Brad Smith (NBC News, 2025).

How accurate it is. Pentagon data from 2024 put Maven's accuracy at identifying objects at about 60%, against 84% for human analysts, and below 30% in poor visibility (Bloomberg, via Military Times). These are old figures for one task, and newer ones haven't been published.

What we can't see. How often armies use AI, how often people overrule it, and how many deaths it's linked to. Most of this is secret, and no one publishes a count.

Can AI make war less deadly?

If AI makes war less deadly

Telling targets apart. People make mistakes in war too. A review of thousands of real cases of civilian harm found patterns that repeat, and ways AI could help reduce them (estimated, CNA, 2022). That's what could happen, not what has been measured.

Human judgement. People can learn to question AI. In one test, West Point cadets followed wrong AI advice 3.9% of the time, against 9% for similar members of the public (2026, not yet peer reviewed). It was a simple task, far from real combat.

Who's responsible. A system can keep a record of what it recommended and why. That could make mistakes easier to investigate than a decision made under fire.

How wars grow. Better information could give leaders a clearer picture and fewer false alarms in a crisis.

If AI makes war more deadly

Telling targets apart. AI can be wrong and sure of itself. In a 2021 US Air Force test, a targeting system was right only about 1 time in 4, but rated itself right 9 times in 10 (Defense One, 2021). More targets can also mean more harm. Human Rights Watch told a US hearing in 2026 that the Pentagon hasn't shared evidence AI reduces civilian harm (Responsible Statecraft).

Human judgement. Being in charge isn't the same as having time to think. A person given seconds to review a machine's suggestion may not be able to challenge it.

Who's responsible. Blame can slip between the people who supplied the data, built the tool and gave the order. On the first day of the 2026 US-Iran war, a US strike hit a girls' school in Minab, Iran, killing more than 150 people, most of them children. A UN fact-finding mission found old intelligence still listed the school as a military site, and that no one properly checked before the strike (UN, 2026). Whether AI picked the school hasn't been made public. Former officials blame the people who supplied the data, and Palantir, which runs the targeting system, says it isn't responsible for the data (Bloomberg, 2026). Reports said the US team assessing civilian harm at the command running the war had been cut from 10 people to one (Politico, via Military Times).

How wars grow. In simulated nuclear crises run by King's College London in 2026, AI models playing against each other never chose to back down, and threats more often led to further escalation (King's College London, 2026). Games aren't real crises (War on the Rocks), but they show what can happen when machines on both sides move faster than people can follow.

The savings are estimates of what could happen. The harms are reported cases. They can't be compared directly.

What nobody knows yet

  • How much AI is really behind strikes. It matters because we can't judge what we can't see. Published investigations into strikes that went wrong would help.
  • Whether AI reduces civilian deaths. Armies say it can, and critics say the evidence isn't there. Published before and after figures would settle it.
  • Whether people really check what AI tells them. Some studies find people follow AI too readily, and others find they reject it, even when it's right (Journal of Conflict Resolution, 2026). Studies of real operations would tell us.
  • Whether AI makes wars more likely, or harder to stop. There's very little real-world evidence. It matters because a mistake between nuclear powers can't be undone.

Our view

War is about causing harm, so the real question is who that harm falls on. History shows that the people with the least power get the least attention, and what happens to them often comes to light long after, sometimes decades later. With any technology we weigh the benefits against the risks and the losses. In war, losses on the other side count for less. AI could make strikes more accurate and save lives. That's a fair argument, but it relies on armies valuing the lives of civilians on the other side, and that isn't always true. So on the current path, we think AI is more likely to make war more dangerous for civilians than less. We'd change our mind if armies and the companies that supply them were open about how decisions are made, what limits apply, and what their tests and simulations show. We're a site focused on AI's risks, so we look hardest at what could go wrong.

What's being done

  • Governments and the UN. In September 2026, 76 countries backed negotiating a binding law on autonomous weapons, after three years of UN talks. Human Rights Watch says Russia and the United States weakened the final text. A UN review conference in November 2026 was due to decide whether negotiations begin. The head of the UN and the president of the International Committee of the Red Cross have jointly called for a ban on autonomous weapons that target people or whose effects can't be predicted.
  • Investigators. A UN fact-finding mission found the Minab school strike was an indiscriminate attack, caused by a "reckless failure of target verification". US lawmakers have asked the Pentagon whether AI was used to pick the school. Much of the rest of what's known comes from journalists, researchers, whistleblowers and groups like Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch. Armies rarely say when AI played a part in a strike.
  • AI companies. Some set limits on military use and others have removed them. OpenAI signed a Pentagon deal in 2026, and said it kept the same limits Anthropic had asked for. Few details of these deals are public, so no one outside can check whether the limits hold.

How to keep AI in war under human control

  • Governments. Agree a binding law that bans weapons which target people or can't be predicted, and requires real human control over the rest. Publish investigations when strikes go wrong. Keep staff whose job is protecting civilians.
  • Companies that build AI. Say clearly what their tools can and can't be used for in war, publish how they perform, and allow independent testing.

What you can do

  1. Ask where your organisation's AI goes. If you buy, sell or invest in AI, ask whether it has military uses and what limits apply. Asking the question often starts the conversation.
  2. Back a binding law on autonomous weapons. Tell your representatives it matters to you.
  3. Send this page to someone who works with AI, or who has never thought about its role in war.
