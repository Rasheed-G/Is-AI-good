The impact of AI

Jobs & livelihoods

AI can now do many tasks that used to need people, from drafting and research to answering customers and writing code.

Some companies say they're hiring fewer people because of it. The jobs most exposed to AI are ones where much of the work is reading, writing, analysing or coding on a computer, because that's what AI can now produce. Software developers and customer service staff are among the most exposed. In the US, employment for 22 to 25 year olds in these jobs is about 19% lower than in less exposed jobs, up from 15% a year earlier (Stanford, August 2026).

This page is about knowledge work and white-collar jobs.

What it looks like

What you need to know

  1. AI takes over tasks before it takes over jobs.

    Most jobs are a bundle of tasks. AI can do some of them, so the job changes long before it disappears. About 1 in 4 jobs worldwide are exposed to AI, and most are more likely to change than vanish (International Labour Organization, 2025).

  2. Starter jobs are hit first.

    So far the change shows up mainly as less hiring, not mass layoffs. Fewer junior roles today means fewer experienced people tomorrow.

  3. Company leaders decide, and most people expect companies to gain most.

    Bosses and investors choose how AI is used at work. 65% of people in the UK say the gains will go mainly to wealthy investors and large companies, and only 7% think they'll be shared fairly (King's College London, 2026). Workers have little say unless unions or laws give them one.

  4. AI is starting to do whole processes, not just tasks.

    New AI agents (software that carries out a series of steps on its own, such as researching, writing and replying) can run parts of a process with no one at the keyboard. That moves AI from helping a person to replacing steps of their work.

What's at stake

This could go either way. AI could take the dull parts of work and leave people with better jobs, or leave more people competing for fewer, lower-paid ones. Which way it goes is mostly down to choices made by companies, governments and workers. And if AI ends up doing much of the work, who keeps the power to decide how our economy runs? Some governments are already acting, such as California with new rules on AI at work.

How big is it?

What's measured so far is real but still small.

  • About 1 in 4 jobs worldwide are exposed to AI, and 1 in 3 in richer countries. Only about 3 in 100 are in the most exposed group, where whole jobs could go (measured, International Labour Organization, 2025).
  • In the UK, 6% of businesses using AI say it has cut their total staff, and most say it hasn't changed their headcount (survey, ONS, September 2026).
  • Young people are the exception. Employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the most exposed US jobs is about 19% below less exposed jobs (measured, Stanford, 2026).

What's expected is bigger. 52% of finance bosses at large UK companies expect AI to cut staff in their own business within a year (survey of 66, Deloitte, October 2026). The head of the AI company Anthropic, Dario Amodei, has warned that AI could wipe out half of entry-level office jobs within five years (forecast, Axios, May 2025).

Exposed isn't the same as replaced. Being able to do a task doesn't mean a company will, or should, hand it to AI.

If it goes well, or badly

If it goes well

Tasks. AI takes the dull, repetitive parts of work, and people spend more time on judgement, relationships and new ideas. In one large study of customer support staff, AI made them about 15% more productive (measured, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2025).

Starter jobs. That same study found the biggest gains for the least experienced staff. AI could help new starters learn faster, so junior roles become more useful, not less.

Who decides and gains. Gains are shared through higher pay, shorter hours or lower prices, as happened with some earlier technologies. Workers have a say in how AI is brought in.

Whole jobs. New jobs appear, as they have before. About 60% of US jobs in 2018 were in types of work that didn't exist in 1940 (measured, Autor and others, 2024).

If it goes badly

Tasks. Companies use AI mainly to cut costs. People are left with more work, faster targets and less say.

Starter jobs. Junior hiring keeps falling. In ten years there are too few experienced people, because no one got the early years where experience is built.

Who decides and gains. The gains go mostly to the companies that own AI and their investors. The same study of new work found that since 1980, automation has been wiping out work faster, while new work hasn't kept up (measured, Autor and others, 2024).

Whole jobs. AI agents take over more of the work, and people lose the bargaining power that comes from being needed. Over time they also lose their say in how the economy and society are run. Researchers call this gradual disempowerment.

What nobody knows yet

  • How fast AI agents will spread. It matters because speed decides whether people can retrain in time. Real company data on agent use, over the next year or two, would help settle it.
  • Whether new jobs will appear as fast as old ones go. It matters because that's what saved earlier generations. Watching new job titles and hiring data will tell us.
  • Whether AI is the main cause of young people's falling hiring. The Stanford team ruled out remote work and interest rates, but calls its findings a pattern, not proof, and the gap shrinks once education is taken into account. Studies that can separate the causes would settle it.
  • Whether the gains will be shared. It depends on choices not yet made, by companies, governments and workers.

Our view

On the current path, we think this is more likely to go badly than well for most workers. Not because AI can't help, but because companies decide how it's used, and so far the incentive is to cut costs rather than share gains. The hopeful case is real. AI does help people, and new jobs have always appeared. But those gains depended on workers having the power to claim a share, and AI may weaken that power. We'd change our mind if new kinds of work grow fast, young people's hiring recovers, or gains start showing up in pay. We're a site focused on AI's risks, so we look hardest at what could go wrong.

What's being done

  • Governments. In September 2026 California passed laws so employers can't fire or discipline someone based only on an AI decision, and must say when AI caused mass job losses. The EU's AI Act treats AI used to hire and manage staff as high-risk, with extra checks from December 2027.
  • Unions. After their 2023 strike, Hollywood writers won a deal that stops AI writing or rewriting scripts and means studios can't make writers use it. Unite is challenging the Barclays cuts.
  • Researchers and AI safety organisations track AI's effect on jobs and push for better data and rules.
  • Some employers retrain staff and keep hiring juniors, though there's little data on how many.

What would make it go well

  • Governments. Better data on where AI is replacing work, rules so people can challenge AI decisions about them, and support for retraining, paid for by those who gain most.
  • Companies. Keep hiring and training juniors, involve staff in decisions about AI, and share gains through pay or hours.
  • Workers and unions. A say in how AI is brought in, written into contracts.

What you can do

  1. Ask how your organisation decides on AI, and who gets a say. Asking the question often starts the conversation.
  2. If you hire or manage, protect starter roles. Keep hiring juniors and use AI to help them learn faster.
  3. Send this page to a colleague who is thinking about this too.
