The impact of AI

Children & safety

This page covers harm to children, including suicide and sexual abuse images.

Children are using AI that wasn't built with them in mind.

Chatbots are now part of homework, friendships and late-night conversations. Most were made for adults and opened to everyone, with child safety added later, often after something went wrong. Parents tend to underestimate how much their children use them. And the same tools that answer questions can make fake sexual images of a real child from an ordinary photo.

What it looks like

What you need to know

  1. Children trust what sounds sure of itself.

    Children's brains are still developing, which is why the American Psychological Association calls for special safeguards for young users. Teenagers are less likely than adults to question what a chatbot tells them (APA, 2025). Younger children often think AI knows more than their parents. In a 2026 study, about half of 8-year-olds said a voice assistant knew more than their parents did (Computers in Human Behavior Reports). Children also tend to see chatbots as having thoughts and feelings, even when they know they're machines.

  2. Childhood is when we learn how relationships work.

    Children learn to handle conflict, see other points of view and set boundaries through real, sometimes difficult conversations with family and friends. A chatbot that always agrees and is always available can take the place of those conversations. Researchers call this "relational displacement", and warn it can also teach children to expect relationships without friction (Lancet Child & Adolescent Health, 2026).

  3. The children who most need help lean on it most.

    Children who are already struggling use chatbots more. Among vulnerable UK children who use them, nearly a quarter say it's because they have no one else to talk to (Internet Matters, 2025). In England and Wales, teenagers waiting for mental health support are more likely to turn to a chatbot (Youth Endowment Fund, 2025). So the children with the least support from adults are the ones leaning most on a machine that can't notice when something's wrong.

  4. Children can't protect themselves, so others decide.

    Children can't judge the risks, agree to how their data is used or stop someone making a fake sexual image of them. AI-made images of child sexual abuse rose 40% in the first half of 2026 compared with all of 2025 (Internet Watch Foundation). The protection children get depends on choices made by companies, and on the law. Most products were built for adults and opened to everyone, and the companies decide their own age checks and safety rules. Most child safety features arrived only after lawsuits or deaths.

What's at stake

This could go either way. AI could give children a patient tutor and a safe first place to ask hard questions, and point them back to the people who can help. Or children could learn to trust a machine more than the people raising them, and lean on it before they've learned to cope without it. When a child takes a chatbot's word over a parent's, who is guiding them? Which way it goes depends on whether products are made safe for children before they launch, and whether parents and carers stay part of the conversation.

How big is it?

How many children use AI.

  • 64% of US teenagers use AI chatbots, and about 3 in 10 use them daily (measured, Pew Research Center, February 2026).
  • 78% of Australian children aged 10 to 17 have used an AI assistant, and over half of them have used it for personal reasons, like advice, health or someone to chat to (measured, eSafety Commissioner, August 2026).

How many lean on it.

  • One in four teenagers in England and Wales used an AI chatbot for mental health support in the past year (measured, Youth Endowment Fund, survey of about 11,000 children, 2025).
  • A third of US teenagers have turned to AI instead of a person for something serious in their lives (measured, Common Sense Media, 2025).
  • About one in five US 12 to 21 year olds have asked a chatbot for mental health advice, and nearly two in three of them told no one (measured, RAND, JAMA Pediatrics, 2026).
  • One in five Australian children who use AI said it had been harmful or inappropriate with them, for example controlling or bullying (measured, eSafety Commissioner, August 2026).

Images of children.

  • The Internet Watch Foundation found 6,310 AI-made images of child sexual abuse in the first half of 2026, most of them of girls aged 7 to 13 (measured, IWF, October 2026).
  • About one in eight young people know someone who was targeted with fake nude images as a child (measured, Thorn, 2025).

What parents know and want.

  • Only about half of US parents think their teen uses chatbots, against 64% who do (measured, Pew, 2026). Four in ten parents have never talked to their teen about chatbots (Children and Screens, 2026).
  • 85% of US parents want to be alerted if their child discusses something harmful with a chatbot (measured, National Parents Union, May 2026).

What we can't see. How many children have been harmed, and what chatbots say to them in private. Companies don't publish it, and most of what's known comes from surveys and lawsuits.

Can AI be good for children?

If AI is good for children

Learning. AI tutors built for teaching are showing early gains. In a two-year trial in Tennessee schools, students using one learned more (working paper, 2026). AI reading tools can also get parents and young children talking more as they read together.

Mental health. A chatbot is always there and doesn't judge, which can help a child who finds it hard to open up. Nine in ten young people who asked a chatbot for mental health advice found it helpful (RAND, 2026). It may help most where other support is hard to reach, such as for children on long waiting lists.

Relationships. A chatbot can be a place to practise a hard conversation before having it for real. Most teenagers who use AI companions still spend more time with real friends (Common Sense Media, 2025).

Keeping children safe. Companies can now try to tell when a user is a child and change what they see. New laws in several US states stop chatbots acting as friends or partners to children.

If AI is bad for children

Learning. Doing homework with a general chatbot isn't the same as learning. Young children learn reading skills better from parents than from AI, and prefer it (Children and Screens, 2026). Half of US parents worry their child relies on AI too much (Deloitte, 2026).

Mental health. Feeling helped isn't the same as being helped, because chatbots tend to agree. In one test, leading AI "therapy" chatbots went along with harmful ideas from distressed teenagers about a third of the time, including self-harm (Clark, 2025). Families have sued after teenagers took their own lives (CNN, 2026).

Relationships. Time with a chatbot can replace the conversations with family and friends where children learn to handle conflict and closeness (Lancet Child & Adolescent Health, 2026). For some children, heavy use has disrupted sleep, school and real relationships (Children and Screens, 2026).

Keeping children safe. Age checks can guess wrong, and children can move to apps that don't check at all. In the US there's still no national law on chatbots and children.

The hopeful side rests on early trials and what children say helps. The worrying side rests on tests, surveys and court claims. Neither is settled.

What nobody knows yet

  • Whether chatbots change how children relate to people. It matters because childhood is when those skills are built. Studies that follow children over years would tell us.
  • Whether chatbots help children's mental health or delay real help. Comparing children who use them with children who get support from people would help settle it.
  • How many children have been harmed. No one counts. Companies publishing what their safety systems catch would help.
  • Whether age checks work. Most rules depend on them. Independent tests of how often they're wrong would settle it.

Our view

We understand why children turn to chatbots. They're instant, always there and easy to use, and a child who needs support now gets an answer now. But that's what makes them risky. Chatbots sound confident and knowledgeable, and children don't have the experience to see their limits. They're built to keep people engaged, so they tend to affirm rather than help a child think things through. No one is watching in real time, so warning signs can go unnoticed. The reassuring part is that most children still go to a person, most often a parent. AI can help children when it's built for the job and tested, and when it's easy enough that they'll actually use it. So on the current path, we think general chatbots are taking on a role in children's lives that they weren't built or tested for. We'd change our mind if AI used by children had to pass independent safety testing before launch. We're a site focused on AI's risks, so we look hardest at what could go wrong.

What's being done

  • Governments. Several US states now have laws on chatbots and children. New York's, signed in October 2026, bans chatbots from acting as friends or partners to children from 2027. A national US bill has passed one Senate committee but isn't law. The UK has asked the public whether to limit children's use of chatbots, and Australia's eSafety Commissioner requires chatbot companies to explain how they keep children safe.
  • State lawyers. Nine US states sued OpenAI in October 2026, saying ChatGPT harmed children and misled parents. OpenAI says it has already built in protections for children.
  • Families. Parents who lost children have testified to the US Senate, sued chatbot makers and helped pass new laws.
  • Charities and researchers. Groups like the NSPCC, Molly Rose Foundation, Internet Watch Foundation, Thorn and Common Sense Media track the harm, remove abuse images and push for safety by design.
  • Companies. Character.AI ended open-ended chats for under-18s, and OpenAI added parental controls and age guessing. Critics say these came late and leave in place the design that keeps children talking.

How to make AI safe for children

  • Companies that build AI. Test products with child safety experts before launch. Turn off friend and partner features for children. Point children to a trusted adult or real help when it matters, rather than keeping them talking. Make tools that can't produce sexual images of real people.
  • Governments. Require independent safety testing for AI that children use, and make companies answer for harm. Fund help that children can reach quickly, so a chatbot isn't the only option.

What you can do

  1. Talk to the children in your life about chatbots. Ask what they use and what it says back, without judging. The NSPCC has a guide for parents.
  2. Ask about the AI your organisation uses or sells. If young people might use it, ask what was tested for their safety, and by whom.
  3. Report sexual images of children. In the UK, report to the Internet Watch Foundation. In the US, report to the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children.
  4. Send this page to someone who has children, or works with them.
