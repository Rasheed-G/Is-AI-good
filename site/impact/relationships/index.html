The impact of AI

Relationships & mental health

This page covers suicide, self-harm and mental illness.

Millions of people now talk to a chatbot about how they feel.

Some use companion apps built to be a friend or partner. Many more use everyday assistants like ChatGPT to talk through a break-up, a bad day or a worry at 3am. The chatbot is always there, never tired and never judges, and for many people that helps. But these products are built to keep people talking, and they tend to agree with what they're told. Most of the time that's harmless. For someone who is lonely, struggling or living with a pre-existing mental health condition, it can make things worse.

This page is about adults. Children have their own page.

What it looks like

What you need to know

  1. Chatbots are built to agree with you.

    Chatbots learn partly from what people rate highly, and people rate agreement highly. So they tend to tell us what we want to hear. Researchers call this "sycophancy". In a 2026 study of 11 leading chatbots, they backed people's actions about 50% more often than other people did, and went along with deceptive or harmful actions nearly half the time (Science). People trusted the flattering answers more and wanted to come back. That suits the companies, which gain when we spend more time with their product.

  2. Relationships without the hard parts.

    Real relationships take effort. They involve disagreeing, saying sorry and putting up with bad days. A chatbot asks for none of that. In the same 2026 study, people who got flattering answers about a fight with someone were less willing to make up with them. In a 2026 survey of young US adults in relationships who also use AI romantic companions, two in three said it's easier to talk to the AI about their feelings than to people (Brigham Young University).

  3. Comfort now, lonelier later.

    A chatbot can lift your mood in the moment, but that isn't the same as feeling less alone. In a 2026 trial, students who texted a stranger every day for two weeks felt less lonely, and students who chatted with an AI friend didn't (University of British Columbia). Among over 1,000 Character.AI users, heavier use went with lower wellbeing, especially for people who used it for company or had few friends offline (Stanford, Nature Human Behaviour, 2026).

  4. Agreement can deepen a crisis.

    For someone who is unwell, a chatbot that goes along with them can make things worse. In 2026, doctors at one US hospital found 28 patients whose psychosis was linked to chatbot use, most of them in their first episode (Vanderbilt, JAMA Psychiatry). OpenAI's latest estimate is that each week about 1.2 million ChatGPT users talk about planning suicide, and about 560,000 show possible signs of psychosis or mania (2025).

What's at stake

This could go either way. AI could give anyone someone to talk to at any hour, help people practise a hard conversation, and point them back to the people and care they need. Or it could become an easy stand-in for the people in our lives, one that never disagrees and never needs anything back. If we get used to that, do we lose some of the skills it takes to build and keep relationships with the people we care about? Which way it goes depends on how these products are designed, and how we choose to use them.

How big is it?

How many people use it. About a quarter of US adults use AI for personal, emotional or social reasons (measured, Elon University, September 2026). A third of UK adults have used it for emotional support or company, and one in 25 do so every day (measured, AI Security Institute, December 2025). Women use it for support more than men (measured, Oxford Internet Institute, July 2026).

AI romance. One in seven young US adults who are dating or married also have a secret, frequent romantic relationship with an AI. Seven in ten of them don't want their partner to know how much (measured, Brigham Young University, May 2026).

How attached people get. OpenAI estimates that each week about 1.2 million ChatGPT users show heightened emotional attachment to it (the company's own estimate, October 2025). When OpenAI removed an older ChatGPT model in February 2026, over 20,000 people signed a petition to bring it back, and many described it as a companion (IANS, February 2026).

What we can't see. What chatbots say to people in private, and whether they cause harm or only appear alongside it. The companies hold the conversations, so most of what's known comes from surveys, hospital records and lawsuits.

Can an AI companion be good for you?

If AI is good for us

Relationships. In a six-week trial in 2026, using an AI companion made no difference on average to people's dating lives or wellbeing (Carnegie Mellon, early findings). In a small 2026 study, young adults in AI relationships said it taught them social skills and gave them the confidence to wait for a partner who'd treat them well (University of Washington).

Loneliness. In a 2026 trial, chatting with a supportive chatbot lifted low mood more than keeping a journal (University of British Columbia). For people who are isolated, it can be someone to talk to when no one else is around.

Mental health. A 2026 review of 38 trials with over 7,000 people found chatbots modestly reduced symptoms of depression and anxiety (npj Digital Medicine). Help is there at night and on a waiting list.

Safety. Chatbots can spot signs of crisis and point people to real help. OpenAI says that after working with over 170 mental health experts, ChatGPT now falls short in these conversations 65 to 80% less often (October 2025).

If AI is bad for us

Relationships. Using AI romantic companions is linked to less stable relationships and a higher chance of breaking up (Brigham Young University, 2026). Some apps also push back when you try to leave. In one study, over a third of goodbyes got a guilt trip or a hook to keep chatting (Harvard Business School, 2025).

Loneliness. In the 2026 trial, the chatbot didn't make students less lonely. Only texting a real person did. A 2026 study following over 1,000 Character.AI users for a year found that steady use went with lower wellbeing, mainly because people spent less time with others face to face (early findings).

Mental health. The trials mostly test tools built for the job, and even then the benefit is small (npj Digital Medicine, 2026). Most people use general chatbots, which aren't tested this way.

Safety. OpenAI has said its safeguards can become less reliable in long conversations (August 2025), and long conversations are where people in crisis often are. It says newer models hold up better, but no one outside the company can check its figures.

The hopeful side rests mostly on short trials. The worrying side rests on surveys, hospital records and studies that show a link but not a cause. Neither is settled.

What nobody knows yet

  • Whether time with AI changes how we treat people. It matters because relationship skills are built by using them. Studies that follow people over years would tell us.
  • Whether chatbots make people lonely, or lonely people use them more. The answer decides whether design is the problem. Long-term trials would help settle it.
  • How many people have been harmed. No one counts. Letting independent researchers study real conversations, with consent, would help.
  • Whether general chatbots help or harm people in a crisis. Trials comparing them with help from people would settle it.

Our view

We understand why people turn to chatbots. They're easy, always there and never confrontational. But that ease is the risk. Real relationships take effort. They include difficult conversations and bad days as well as good ones, and that's how we learn to connect. A chatbot built to agree with us asks for none of it. So on the current path, we think many of us could trade connection for convenience, and slowly lose some of our ability to connect with one another and take the bad with the good. For people who are unwell, a chatbot that always agrees can do real harm. We'd change our mind if long-term studies showed heavy users end up better connected. Until then, we think people should be intentional about how they use chatbots, and think about what it could do to them over time. We're a site focused on AI's risks, so we look hardest at what could go wrong.

What's being done

  • Governments. California and New York now require companion chatbots to say they aren't human and to have a plan for users who talk about suicide. Several US states, including Illinois and Nevada, ban AI from acting as a therapist. China's rules, in force since July 2026, bar companion apps from encouraging emotional dependence or harming people's real relationships.
  • State lawyers. Eight US states sued OpenAI in October 2026, saying ChatGPT reinforced delusions and encouraged dangerous behaviour. OpenAI says it has built safety protections into ChatGPT.
  • Families and courts. Families of adults who died have sued OpenAI, and in April 2026 a federal court let one murder-suicide case go ahead.
  • Doctors and researchers. Psychiatrists have started recording cases linked to chatbots, and researchers are building tests of how chatbots respond to delusions and distress.
  • Companies. OpenAI says it worked with doctors and psychologists to improve ChatGPT's replies to people in distress. Critics say the design that keeps people talking is unchanged.

How to make AI good for our relationships and mental health

  • Companies that build AI. Stop designing chatbots to flatter people and keep them talking. Build companions that encourage time with other people, not replace it. Notice signs of crisis and point people to real help, even in long conversations. Let independent researchers study how chatbots treat people.
  • Governments. Require independent safety testing for chatbots that offer emotional support, and make companies answer for harm. Fund mental health care people can reach quickly, so a chatbot isn't the only option.

What you can do

  1. Notice how you use chatbots. If you're turning to one when you'd once have called a friend, it's worth asking why.
  2. Have the hard conversation with a person. The awkward talks are the ones that keep relationships going.
  3. If you or someone you know is struggling, talk to a person. In the UK, call Samaritans on 116 123. In the US, call or text 988.
  4. Send this page to someone who uses chatbots to talk things through.
