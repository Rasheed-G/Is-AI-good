The impact of AI

The environment & data centres

AI runs in data centres, huge buildings full of computers that need power and water day and night. Companies are building them faster than ever. Not all of this is AI. Most data centre work is still ordinary storage, streaming and websites. But AI is the fastest-growing part, and the main reason for the building boom.

One question to a chatbot uses very little. But billions of questions a day, and new sites the size of small towns, add up. The places they're built lose water and land. The people living nearby get the noise, the pollution and higher power bills.

What it looks like

What you need to know

  1. One question is small, the total isn't.

    A typical question to Google's Gemini uses about as much electricity as nine seconds of television, and about five drops of water (Google, 2025, its own figures). But data centres' use of electricity rose 17% in 2025, and is expected to double by 2030. The part used for AI is expected to triple (International Energy Agency, 2026).

  2. The cost lands on a few places.

    Worldwide, data centres use a small share of electricity. But they cluster where power, land and tax deals are good. In Ireland they use almost a quarter of the country's electricity. In the eastern US, the grid pays power stations in advance so there's enough electricity in future years, and households pay for it through their bills. The grid's own watchdog says data centres caused most of a $9.3 billion rise in that cost.

  3. Speed beats clean.

    Elon Musk's xAI ran dozens of gas turbines near Memphis without air permits, in mainly Black neighbourhoods, to power its AI quickly. Burning gas gives off fumes that form smog, and soot, both linked to asthma and heart disease. Without a permit, no one checks the pollution controls. It is now being sued.

  4. Much of it is hidden.

    Many data centres are agreed under secrecy deals, so neighbours can't see how much water or power a site will use until it's built. EU law now requires data centres to report their energy and water use, but Ireland, with Europe's biggest share, hadn't enforced it by September 2026.

What's at stake

This could go either way. AI could help cut emissions, by running power grids better, designing better batteries and cutting waste. Or the rush to build locks in new gas power, takes water from dry places and leaves neighbours with the noise and the bills. Which way it goes depends on whether companies are made to build clean, show what they use, and pay their own way. When a town's water or power is at stake, who gets a say?

How big is it?

Electricity.

  • Data centres used about 1.5% of the world's electricity in 2024. The International Energy Agency expects that to roughly double by 2030, to about the electricity Japan uses today. The share of electricity used for AI is expected to about triple (IEA, 2025 and 2026).
  • In the US, data centres used about 5% of all electricity in 2025, more than three times the world average. In Virginia it's over a quarter (Our World in Data, September 2026, from IEA and Ember figures; Electric Power Research Institute, 2026). They could use about 10% to 15% by 2030 (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, 2026).

Water.

  • Data centres use water to keep computers cool, and power stations use more to make their electricity. US data centres used about 66 billion litres directly in 2023, about as much as 600,000 Americans use at home in a year (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, 2024; US Environmental Protection Agency).
  • What matters most is where. A site's water can be a small share of a wet region's supply, or a big one in a dry place or a drought.

Air and health.

  • Where the grid can't keep up, some sites burn gas on site, with turbines or diesel generators. That brings smog and soot to the people nearby.
  • A study paid for by the group suing xAI estimated its Mississippi turbines would cause $30 to $44 million of health harm a year (Southern Environmental Law Center, 2026). It's an estimate, from one side of a court case.

Bills.

  • The grid that serves 65 million people in the eastern US pays power stations in advance to have electricity ready in future years, so the lights stay on at the busiest times. Households pay for this through their bills. For the year from June 2025, that cost became about nine times higher. The grid's independent watchdog put 63% of that rise, about $9.3 billion, down to data centres (Monitoring Analytics, 2025).

What we can't see. Companies report their own totals, in their own ways. Few publish figures site by site. Many local numbers are hidden by secrecy deals, where a council or water company signs an agreement with the developer not to reveal its plans or how much water and power it will use.

Can AI help the climate?

If AI helps the climate

Emissions. AI could cut 3.2 to 5.4 billion tonnes of emissions a year by 2035, more than the 0.4 to 1.6 billion its data centres would add. Mostly by helping power grids take more wind and solar, speeding up meat and dairy alternatives, and helping people share cars and switch to electric ones (London School of Economics, Grantham Institute, 2025).

Energy. Each question keeps getting cheaper to answer. The energy used per AI task is falling faster than anything seen before in energy (IEA, 2026).

Water and air. Cleaner ways to build already exist. Google switched its India site to air cooling after objections.

Who pays. Some places now make data centres pay their own grid costs. Oregon has a separate price for them.

If AI harms the climate

Emissions. Those savings depend on governments steering AI towards them, the study says. The emissions from building data centres are already happening. Microsoft's total emissions, from the power it buys to building its data centres, rose 25% in the year to June 2025, as it built for AI. It says the rise was "driven primarily by the expansion of our datacenter infrastructure", despite its pledge to remove more carbon than it emits by 2030 (Microsoft, 2026).

Energy. When something gets cheaper, people use more of it. Total use keeps rising even as each question gets more efficient. Some sites burn gas because it's quicker than waiting for the grid.

Water and air. Sites go where power and land are cheap, including dry places and poorer neighbourhoods, as with xAI near Memphis.

Who pays. Neighbours get the noise and the pollution, and households in the eastern US are paying for the extra power capacity.

The savings are estimates of what could happen. The emissions are measured now. They can't be compared directly.

What nobody knows yet

  • Whether AI's climate savings will happen. They're estimates. Measured cuts in real power grids, farms and transport would tell us.
  • How big the build-out will really be. Some planned sites will never be built, and forecasts vary widely. Real use, reported each year, would tell us.
  • How much water and power each site really uses. Most figures are private. Required, published reports, site by site, would tell us.
  • How much this adds to bills. Experts disagree on the share data centres cause. Independent reviews by regulators would settle it.

Our view

On the current path, we think AI's build-out is costing the places it's built, and the climate, more than it needs to. Not because it has to. Clean power, air cooling and quieter designs exist. But companies racing to build gain from speed and secrecy, so some burn gas without permits, build in dry places and sign deals that keep neighbours in the dark. AI may help the climate later, but the costs are being paid now. We'd change our mind if companies publish what each site uses, build on clean power, pay their own grid costs, and AI's promised savings show up in measured emissions. We're a site focused on AI's risks, so we look hardest at what could go wrong.

What's being done

  • Governments. The EU requires data centres to report their energy and water use, though enforcement is patchy. Amsterdam has stopped new data centres until at least 2030. Virginia taxes data centres' electricity from July 2026, and Oregon charges them their own electricity price (Reuters, 2026). Tulare County, California paused new sites to protect its groundwater.
  • Courts. The NAACP is suing xAI over its turbines in Mississippi under US clean air law. Neighbours in Dowagiac are suing over noise.
  • Communities. Residents have objected, packed meetings and voted, and some projects have been dropped or changed. Over 7,000 people objected to one site in Larbert, Scotland.
  • AI companies report their totals each year, and some have promised clean power and to put back more water than they use. Their emissions are still rising.

How to make AI cleaner

  • Governments. Make every large data centre publish its power and water use, site by site. Ban secrecy deals with local councils. Make data centres pay their own grid costs, and require clean power and permits before they open.
  • Companies that build AI. Build where clean power and water are plentiful, use air cooling in dry places, and report emissions honestly, including from building the sites.

What you can do

  1. At work, ask your AI supplier what it uses. Ask for its energy and water figures and where its data centres are. Prefer suppliers who publish them. Use smaller, simpler tools for simple jobs.
  2. If a data centre is planned near you, ask before it's approved. Ask your council for the site's expected water, power and noise figures, and whether a secrecy deal is involved. Planning objections have changed or stopped projects.
  3. Back rules that make data centres show what they use and pay their own way. Tell your representatives it matters to you.
  4. Send this page to someone near a planned site, or who buys AI at work.
