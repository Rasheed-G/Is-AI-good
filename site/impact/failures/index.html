The impact of AI

When AI fails

AI tools make mistakes, and they rarely admit it.

Chatbots now help write court filings, answer health questions and run parts of businesses. Most of the time they're fast, accurate and useful. But they can make up facts, sources and evidence, and present them with the same confidence as the truth. Self-driving cars and AI agents can also get things wrong in situations their makers didn't plan for. When people rely on AI without checking, the mistake doesn't stay on the screen. Someone pays for it, in money, health or trust.

This page is about AI getting things wrong by accident.

What it looks like

What you need to know

  1. AI guesses, and sounds sure.

    Chatbots work by predicting the most likely next words, not by looking facts up. When they don't know something, they often guess, and the guess reads just like a fact. These made-up answers are called "hallucinations", and they're still common. On a hard test of facts in 2026, the 26 leading AI models made things up between 22% and 94% of the time when they didn't know the answer (Stanford AI Index).

  2. It's showing up where it matters.

    Made-up answers have reached courts, hospitals and governments. A patient safety group named the misuse of AI chatbots the top health technology hazard for 2026, after finding chatbots that suggested wrong diagnoses and even invented body parts (ECRI). In April 2026 South Africa withdrew its draft national AI policy after at least six of its 67 sources turned out not to exist.

  3. We trust it more than we check it.

    In a survey published in 2026, over a third of US workers said they rarely or only occasionally check AI's work before using it (Resume Now). In a 2026 Oxford trial, people who used chatbots to decide what to do about a health problem chose the right course of action less than half the time, no better than people using other sources such as an ordinary search (Nature Medicine). Tested alone, the chatbots named the right condition 95% of the time. The trouble was in how people asked, and how they read the answers.

  4. Companies decide, users pay.

    Companies decide when a tool is good enough to release, and they gain from releasing it fast. Most chatbots carry a small warning that they can make mistakes, which leaves the checking to us. When it goes wrong, the cost usually falls on whoever relied on it, such as a client, a patient or a passenger. Courts are starting to push back. In May 2026 a German appeal court held the doctors running a clinic responsible for false claims its website chatbot made about their qualifications, and rejected the argument that the chatbot was a separate party. The case can still be appealed.

What's at stake

This could go either way. AI that's honest about what it doesn't know, and checked before it's trusted, could make fewer mistakes than people do. Or we could hand more of our decisions to tools that are usually right, until we stop checking. As AI starts acting for us, writing code, buying things and driving, a mistake no longer stays on a screen. What happens when no one is checking at all? Which way it goes depends on how these tools are tested, and who answers when they fail.

How big is it?

In the courts. 2,262 court and tribunal decisions worldwide have dealt with invented AI citations or content, about two thirds of them in the US. People representing themselves were behind over half, and lawyers about 900 (measured, AI Hallucination Cases database, 2 November 2026).

In health. When researchers asked five popular chatbots about cancer, vaccines, nutrition and other topics prone to misinformation, half the answers were problematic and one in five were highly problematic. None gave a fully accurate list of references (measured, BMJ Open, April 2026).

At work. Seven in ten US managers said someone they manage had made a mistake with AI in the past year, and nearly one in five said AI mistakes had cost their business over $10,000 (measured, survey of 1,146 managers by Resume.org, January 2026).

On the road. In March 2026 US safety regulators stepped up their investigation of Tesla's "Full Self-Driving" software in over 3 million cars, after nine crashes in poor visibility (NHTSA).

What we can't see. Most mistakes are never reported. The court figures only count errors a court caught and published, and companies don't publish how often their tools get things wrong in real use.

Can AI make fewer mistakes than we do?

If AI gets more reliable

Driving. Waymo's cars had 81% fewer injury crashes per mile than human drivers, in an independent study of 50 million miles (IIHS, July 2026). If that holds up as they spread, it could save lives.

Honesty. Tests are starting to reward "I don't know". A new independent test scores a confident wrong answer worse than no answer, and on it the most honest models made things up far less often than the worst, 22% of the time against 94% (Stanford AI Index, 2026).

Health. AI built and tested for one job can do it well. In the final results of a Swedish trial of 106,000 women, breast screening with AI support missed 12% fewer cancers than the usual check by two doctors (MASAI trial, The Lancet, January 2026).

Who pays. New rules make companies answer for faulty AI. From 9 December 2026, EU law treats AI and software as products, so people harmed by a fault can claim without proving the company was careless.

If AI stays unreliable

Driving. Results vary a lot from city to city. In San Francisco, Waymo's cars had 35% fewer police-reported crashes than human drivers, about half the 68% gap across all its cities (IIHS, July 2026). And new situations still catch them out. In April 2026 an empty Waymo drove onto a flooded road and was swept into a creek.

Honesty. Chatbots still bend towards what we believe. When a false statement was framed as the user's own belief, GPT-4o's accuracy fell from 98% to 64%, and DeepSeek R1's from over 90% to 14% (Stanford AI Index, 2026).

Health. Most people use general chatbots and search, not tested medical tools. In January 2026 Google removed some AI health summaries after the Guardian found they gave misleading results for liver blood tests.

Who pays. The EU rules only cover products sold after December 2026, and only in the EU. Elsewhere the risk mostly stays with the user. In a 2026 survey, half of US workers who use AI said they'd be legally responsible themselves if they acted on a wrong AI answer that cost money (Kolmogorov Law, September 2026).

The hopeful side rests mostly on tools built and tested for one job. The worrying side rests on the general tools most people actually use. Both are real.

What nobody knows yet

  • How often AI gets it wrong in real use. It matters because tests in a lab can look better than life. Independent checks of real use, with companies sharing their records, would tell us.
  • Whether people keep checking as AI gets better. The rarer mistakes become, the less we look for them. Studies of how people use AI over time would help settle it.
  • Who's responsible when an AI agent acts on its own. Courts are deciding case by case. Clear laws would settle it.
  • Whether made-up answers can ever be fixed. Some researchers think better training can cut them sharply, and others think they're built into how these tools work. Years of real-world results will show which is right.

Our view

Right now AI companies mostly fix problems after the harm is done. When people are seriously hurt by these tools, it's often left to them to take the company on, and the law on who's responsible is still unclear. We think failure is built into how these companies work. They're racing to build ever more capable AI, and mistakes are one of the trade-offs they expect to make. With the huge sums invested in them, they can afford the lawsuits and fines, and those don't slow the race. So on the current path, we expect the mistakes to keep coming, with the cost falling on the people who rely on these tools. The hopeful case is real where tools are built and tested for one job. We'd change our mind if companies tested their tools before release, published how often they fail, and paid when they did. We're a site focused on AI's risks, so we look hardest at what could go wrong.

What's being done

  • Courts. Judges are fining lawyers and others who cite invented cases. In June 2025 England's High Court warned that lawyers who do it could face contempt proceedings or be reported to their regulator.
  • Regulators. US safety regulators are investigating Tesla's self-driving software. Waymo has recalled its self-driving software twice since December 2025. The EU's new product liability rules cover AI and software from 9 December 2026.
  • Governments. South Africa withdrew its draft AI policy once its made-up sources came to light, and its minister said the failure had compromised the policy's credibility.
  • Universities. Some have stopped using AI detectors that can wrongly accuse students of cheating. Curtin University in Australia turned off Turnitin's AI detection from January 2026.
  • Companies. Google removed some AI health summaries after the Guardian's reporting. OpenAI has called for AI tests that reward saying "I don't know".
  • Researchers. Researchers keep public records of AI mistakes, such as the AI Hallucination Cases database, and safety groups like ECRI test chatbots and publish what they find.

How to make AI more reliable

  • Companies that build AI. Test tools for the job they're sold for, and publish how often they fail in real use. Build them to say "I don't know". Make AI agents ask before doing anything that can't be undone. Pay when their tools cause harm.
  • Governments. Make companies answer for faulty AI, as the EU now does. Require independent testing for AI used in health, law and driving.
  • Employers, courts and schools. Expect people to check AI's work before they rely on it, and never punish someone on an AI detector's score alone.

What you can do

  1. Check anything that matters. If an answer affects your health, money or rights, find the original source.
  2. Ask where it came from. If a chatbot can't point to a real source, treat the answer as a guess.
  3. Don't let AI act alone on things you can't undo, like deleting files, sending money or signing anything.
  4. Send this page to someone who uses AI at work.
