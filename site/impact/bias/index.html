The impact of AI

Bias & discrimination

AI now helps decide who gets a job interview, what care they get, or a second look from the police.

It learns from past decisions, and we know many of those were unfair. When researchers sent employers matched job applications, ethnic minority applicants in Britain had to send 60% more to get a positive reply than white British applicants. For Black and South Asian applicants, the gap was no smaller than in the late 1960s (Nuffield College, Oxford, 2019). AI trained on decisions like these can learn the same unfairness, and repeat it for thousands of people at once.

What it looks like

These cases were decided in 2024 and 2025. Proving bias takes years, so the clearest cases are about tools from a few years ago.

What you need to know

  1. The obvious bias has changed direction, the hidden kind hasn't gone.

    Until recently, AI models given identical CVs tended to pick the white and male names (University of Washington, 2024). Now, given identical CVs with only the name changed, all 22 chatbots tested picked the woman more often (2026). Companies have pushed their models away from the old pattern, sometimes too far. But GPT-5 still gave different medical advice for identical cases depending on the patient's background (2025, not yet peer reviewed).

  2. Same facts, different answers.

    Researchers gave AI models the same statements written in African American English and in standard English, and asked what the person who said them was like. The models described the African American English speakers as lazy and stupid, and in a pretend murder trial sentenced them to death more often (28% against 23%). Race was never mentioned (Nature, 2024, testing models up to 2023).

  3. A tool can pass its own check and still be unfair.

    Pymetrics, an AI hiring tool used by 156 large employers, looked fair when its maker added up results across all jobs. Checked one job at a time, about 1 in 10 jobs showed the warning sign US regulators use for unfair hiring. That's a reason to look closer, not proof. New York City's audit rules use the added-up method (Stanford and others, 2026).

  4. One tool, many employers, little say.

    A biased person affects the people in front of them and around them. A biased tool affects everyone it screens, at every employer that uses it, the same way. The companies that build and buy it decide whether to test it, and the people screened out may never know a tool was involved.

What's at stake

This could go either way. AI can be tested for bias in ways a person can't, and companies have already pushed back the most obvious kind. Or the bias goes underground, into tools that pass their checks while still treating people unequally. Which way it goes depends on whether companies are made to test properly, and whether people can find out and push back. As more decisions about us move into systems few people can see inside, who holds them to account?

How big is it?

In real use, where we can see it.

  • In the UK, the government's own check of its Universal Credit fraud tool found it picked people for checks unevenly by age, disability, marital status and nationality. The department says this raises no immediate concerns (reported by the Guardian, December 2024).

In tests of the newest AI, the picture is mixed.

  • The old name bias has dropped, and sometimes reversed. In 2024 tests, AI models picked the white-sounding name in 85% of comparisons between identical CVs, and Black men's CVs lost in up to 100% (University of Washington, 2024). In 2026 tests of 14 chatbots, models from 2024 onwards showed no pro-white gap, and some slightly favoured Black applicants (Gao and others, 2026, not yet peer reviewed). In tests of 22 chatbots, every one favoured women's CVs over identical men's, in 57% of choices (PeerJ Computer Science, 2026).
  • Across 1.7 million answers, nine AI models gave different medical advice for identical cases depending on whether the patient was labelled Black, homeless or LGBTQ+, often sending them for more urgent care, invasive tests or mental-health checks (Nature Medicine, 2025). A follow-up found GPT-5 no better than GPT-4o, and on some measures worse. It sent several LGBTQ+ groups for mental-health checks in every case (Mount Sinai, 2025, not yet peer reviewed).
  • More than half of England's councils use AI tools in social care. When researchers tested one widely available model, Google's Gemma, on identical care notes, it described men's needs as more serious than women's (London School of Economics, 2025).

What we can't see. Most companies don't share the data that would show what happens to real applicants or patients. The Pymetrics study only happened because the vendor agreed to share its data. Its tool scores online games, not CVs, and getting through only put someone on a list for a person to review.

Can AI be fairer than us?

If AI makes things fairer

Compared with people. People are biased too, as the job application tests above show. A study of US bail decisions found an algorithm could jail up to 42% fewer people with no rise in crime, while narrowing racial gaps (estimated, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2018).

Finding bias. You can test AI in ways you can't test a person. Change only the name on a CV and see what happens.

Fixing it. Once found, bias can be changed. The newest chatbots no longer show the old name bias, though some now lean the other way. The makers of a US health tool cut its racial bias by 86%, on one measure, after researchers pointed it out (Science, 2019).

Challenging a decision. A tool's decisions can be logged, checked and explained, which is more than you get from a person's gut feeling.

If AI makes things less fair

Compared with people. AI makes old unfairness look objective, and people tend to trust a computer's answer. Detroit police arrested Robert Williams on a face match and a weak photo line-up.

Finding bias. Fixing the obvious bias can hide the rest. In the Nature study, training made models less openly biased while the hidden bias stayed, and GPT-5 showed as much hidden bias in medical advice as GPT-4o. Tools can also pass a check that adds results up, as in the Pymetrics study.

Fixing it. Fixes often come only when someone forces them. Detroit changed its rules after a lawsuit, and Meta was told to fix its job ads only after campaigners complained.

Challenging a decision. People often don't know a tool judged them. Pa Edrissa Manjang "should never have had to bring a legal claim" to understand the processes that affected his work, said the head of the UK's equality watchdog.

The human and AI figures on this page come from different tests, so they can't be compared directly.

What nobody knows yet

  • Whether the newest AI is less biased, or just better at hiding it. The old obvious bias is down in tests, but the hidden kind isn't, in the few tests of the newest models. Tests of real decisions made with new chatbots would settle it.
  • How biased the tools in real use are. Most data is private. The Pymetrics study needed a vendor to volunteer its data. Required, published audits would tell us.
  • Whether AI is fairer or less fair than the people it replaces. Both are biased. Studies that compare the two on the same decisions would tell us.
  • Whether the law will be enforced. New rules exist, but early checks found them barely enforced (see What's being done).

Our view

On the current path, we think AI is more likely to make unfairness worse than to fix it. Not because it has to. The big AI companies have pushed back the most visible bias, under public pressure, which is real progress. But the firms that sell and buy tools that decide about people gain from speed and low cost, and face little pressure to look for what's harder to see. The bias that's left is less visible, and the main checks we have can miss it. Much of the bias made public so far was found by researchers, journalists or people suing. We'd change our mind if independent, job-by-job checks of tools in real use show fair results, and people can find out and challenge AI decisions about them. We're a site focused on AI's risks, so we look hardest at what could go wrong.

What's being done

  • Governments. Since 2023, New York City has required employers to have AI hiring tools checked for bias each year and to publish the results. A New York State audit found the city's enforcement "ineffective" (December 2025), and researchers say the check itself can miss bias. Since October 2025, California has made clear that its anti-discrimination law applies to AI used in hiring and promotion. The EU's AI Act treats AI used for hiring and lending as high-risk, with extra checks from December 2027. In the UK, existing equality law already applies, and the equality watchdog backed the Uber Eats case.
  • Courts. Derek Mobley says he was turned down for more than a hundred jobs through Workday's software, and is suing. A US court has let his age claim go ahead for applicants over 40, and said the software maker, not just the employer, could be held responsible. Workday denies the claims. A settlement forced changes at Detroit police.
  • Equality bodies. In 2025, equality bodies in the Netherlands and France ruled Facebook's job ad system discriminated by sex and told Meta to change it. The Dutch ruling isn't legally binding.
  • Researchers test tools and publish what they find, when they can get the data.
  • AI companies have pushed back the most obvious bias in their newest chatbots, sometimes too far, as the 2026 tests show.

How to make AI fair

  • Governments. Independent bias tests, job by job, before tools are used for big decisions, results made public, a right to know when AI judged you and to challenge it, and rules that are actually enforced.
  • Companies that build AI. Test for hidden bias as well as obvious bias, publish how the tool performs for each group, and share data with researchers.

What you can do

  1. Before you use an AI tool that judges people, ask for its bias test. Ask who it was tested on and how it performs for each group, role by role, not just overall. If the supplier can't say, that tells you something. Prefer suppliers who publish these results.
  2. Think through what the tool is really doing. Where could bias come in, from the data, from stand-ins like postcode, CV gaps or the way someone writes, or from who checks the results? Then ask what you can do in your role to reduce it, such as keeping a person who can overrule it, and checking the results after it goes live.
  3. Send this page to someone who buys or uses these tools.
