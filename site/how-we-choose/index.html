How we choose stories

What we look for

Real examples of harm AI is causing, or risks it is creating, right now. Each story has to point to something real, such as a named person, organisation, event or research finding. Every story links to where it came from. Each one needs a source we can trust, such as a news outlet, a court, a regulator or a study. We write for thoughtful, busy readers who want the facts.

How a story gets here

  1. 1

    Find. We scan news sites, news alerts, YouTube and Reddit for stories about AI.

  2. 2

    Filter. A quick keyword check and an AI tool drop anything clearly off topic.

  3. 3

    Judge. A second AI tool scores each story on how far we can trust the source, what people actually lost, whether it shows more than one case, whether it is worth a busy reader's time, and which theme it fits. Only stories that clear our bar are kept.

  4. 4

    Summarise. AI writes our own title and a short summary that says only what the source says.

  5. 5

    Remove repeats. We drop any story we already have.

  6. 6

    Publish. Stories that clear the bar go live straight away. Sensitive ones go live hidden, and a few wait until a person has looked at them.

What we leave out

Hype, statistics with no source, adverts, opinion with no real event behind it, warnings about what AI might do one day, and stories where AI is only mentioned in passing.

Our themes

Every story goes in one of 13 themes.

How we chose them

We started with what people worry about most, and what actually changes their minds. We read four large studies.

  • Seismic asked 10,000 people in five countries how worried they are about AI.
  • Anthropic interviewed more than 80,000 AI users in 159 countries about what they fear.
  • A US study of 1,063 people tested which messages would move them most.
  • Social Change Lab measured what one short news story about an AI harm did to 3,467 people in the UK.

From that we built the list. Each theme is a harm happening today, not a prediction. Each one is named by what it does to people, not by the technology. And each needs real cases we can find and source.

Then we checked it against MIT's AI Risk Repository, which maps the risks researchers have found. Our themes cover all seven of its areas.

A few worries are not themes yet. Losing skills by relying on AI, and AI's benefits going to a few, are two we are watching. Weak rules and oversight sit behind every harm, so each theme page covers them. Most of this research comes from the US, UK and Europe, and we will keep checking the list against what our readers tell us.

The 13 themes

  • Jobs & livelihoods. How AI is changing work, pay and who gets hired.
  • Bias & discrimination. When AI treats people unfairly because of who they are.
  • Environment & data centres. What AI costs the planet and the people living next to it.
  • AI and war. How AI is used in war, weapons and military targeting.
  • Children & safety. How AI puts children at risk, and what's being done about it.
  • Relationships & mental health. How AI chatbots and companions affect people's relationships and mental health.
  • When AI fails. When AI fails and people pay the price, in money, health or trust.
  • Hacking & AI acting autonomously. When AI is used to break into systems, or acts beyond what people told it to do.
  • Automated decision making. When a computer decides about your money, benefits, services or rights, with no person checking.
  • Misinformation. How AI spreads false information and fakes that mislead people.
  • Surveillance & privacy. How AI watches people and uses their personal data.
  • Scams & fraud. How criminals use AI to trick people out of money.
  • Image abuse. When AI is used to make fake sexual or humiliating images of real people.

When a story fits two themes

  • The harm decides, not the tool. A deepfake used to steal money goes in Scams, one made to mislead voters goes in Misinformation, and a sexual fake goes in Image abuse.
  • If a child is harmed, the story goes in Children & safety.
  • Bias is for stories where one group is treated worse than others. A tool that fails everyone goes in When AI fails.
  • Stories where people pushed back and won, such as a ruling or a new law, go in the theme of the harm.

Sensitive stories

Stories about things like suicide, children or abuse stay hidden until you choose to show them, and carry a label. If a picture or video could itself cause harm, or a child could be identified, a person reviews the story before it goes live.

What this means

Most stories go live without a person reading them first, so mistakes can get through. When we find one, we remove it.

Why it's one-sided

We collect stories about harm and risk, including ones where people pushed back and won, such as a court ruling or a company changing course. AI does a lot of good too, and that gets plenty of attention. We focus on the risks because they get the least.
