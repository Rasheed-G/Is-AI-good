Our theory of change, version 4. Updated 26 October 2026.

The whole plan takes about 50 minutes to read. The summary and the journey take about 5.

The theory in brief

  • Who we are: Is AI Good? helps experienced professionals from outside tech take their first steps into AI safety, through our website, content and community.
  • The problem: AI is getting more powerful faster than the work to make it safe. The field is short of experienced people who can build, run and grow organisations, and has mostly been treated as a technical problem.
  • Who we help: experienced professionals from non-technical backgrounds who want their work to matter.
  • Why us: other help is mostly technical, often selective, and assumes you already know the field or its networks. We make the first step accessible, and reach people outside the field's usual networks.
  • The journey: eight steps, from noticing the harms AI is causing today, to believing the risk is serious and can be tackled, to seeing where they fit and taking a first step.
  • Where we stop: at that first step: joining an organisation, starting something, or campaigning. Others take it from there.
  • The goal: reduce the chance and severity of AI permanently damaging humanity's wellbeing or long-term future.
  • Where we work: globally and online. Most of our evidence comes from the US and UK.

Numbers in brackets, e.g. [1], point to the sources in Appendix A at the end of this document.

AI safety is the work of making sure advanced AI doesn't cause serious or permanent harm, through research, new organisations, policy and public pressure.

1. Problem

The problem. AI is becoming more powerful faster than the work to make it safe, and not enough is being done to close the gap. The field working on AI safety is small: about 1,100 full-time staff [1], against about 19,000 building the most advanced AI [2]. (For context, in 2025 the largest tech companies spent over $400 billion on capital investment, mostly AI data centres and chips [3], while AI safety received about $0.5–1 billion [4].)

Why. Two things hold the field back.

  • It can't grow fast enough. Safety organisations are short of experienced people to build, run and grow them [5][6], and the largest funder says too few new organisations are being started [7].
  • It has mostly been treated as a technical problem. Most of its training and career guidance focus on technical research and policy [8]. But some of the biggest risks aren't only technical: AI could put power in very few hands, or people could slowly lose their say over the economy and society. These risks are political, economic and social, so tackling them needs more than technical research, including public and political pressure.

Both point the same way: the field needs more experienced people who can start, build and run organisations.

Who's affected. This has the potential to impact everyone globally, now and in the future.

2. Ultimate goal (shared)

Our ultimate goal is to reduce the probability and severity of AI permanently damaging humanity's wellbeing or long-term future.

Shared — we contribute to this; we don't own it alone.

3. Our approach — the bet

The objections this bet must survive are in Section 8.2.

Our core bet: we can contribute to AI safety by getting more of the experienced people described below to work on it, because the field's tightest limit is people who can build and run organisations and bring the public with them, not money or researchers (Section 1).

Our part is the start. We take experienced professionals from knowing little about AI and AI safety to the basics of both: enough about how AI works to make sense of the risks, what the risks are, how the field works, and where someone like them fits. Deeper learning, and the jobs themselves, happen elsewhere: we hand people on to the courses, career services and incubators that take them further, rather than compete with them.

Why we're needed.

  • Free introductions to AI risk exist, but little of it explains how the field works and where someone from business fits [8].
  • Most career help is selective: you have to clear a bar to get in [9], and it expects you to already know the field or its networks [8][10].
  • Others mostly explain AI risk in technical or policy terms. We make it accessible: plain language, no jargon, in our audience's terms (Section 6).
  • We aim to reach people outside the networks the field already draws from, widening who comes in rather than adding to the same pool. We don't have those channels yet: designing and testing them is part of the plan (Section 8, row 3a). (Whether people would have got there anyway is checked in Section 9.2.)

Who we focus on — and why. Experienced professionals from non-technical backgrounds who want their work to matter and make things happen. Wanting their work to matter is something we look for, not a bar people must clear: caring about AI risk specifically is what our work builds (Gate 4, one of eight steps in the journey: Sections 4 and 5).

Everyone gets the same start. At the end of our part they leave by one of three routes: the door they go through next. Whichever route, for most people the first step is learning more about the part that interests them most, before they join, start or campaign. We don't run a separate track for each route; each gets a short add-on where it needs one, and a hand-off to the organisations that take people further.

  • Joining an organisation: professionals from the core business functions — operations, finance, marketing, HR and the others organisations need to grow and deliver. The field has more money and ideas than people to put them to work. Wherever this document says "business professionals", it means this group.
  • Starting something: an organisation, project or programme. Founders, people with a founder mentality, and people who want to build something smaller. Each new organisation, project or programme creates room for more people to work on different solutions. We don't coach people to found. We show starting something as one way to help, and point those who want to towards incubators and funders. So fewer of our people will start things than if we did.
  • Campaigning: community organisers and social activists. Public opinion shapes what governments do, most on visible issues [11], though how much ordinary people's views count is debated: one major US study finds business and wealthy interests decide most outcomes [54], while a rebuttal finds the middle usually gets what it wants anyway [55]. Organisers can turn concern into action at scale. The case is weaker than for the other two routes: nothing yet shows the field is short of organisers rather than money. We keep it because public and political pressure matters to the problem (Section 1), and we expect more funding to go to advocacy (expected, to be checked). We'll ask 3–5 people who run campaigns whether they're short of organisers or of money; if it's money, campaigning moves off our focus.

Honest caveat: this is our biggest bet — well argued, not yet proven.

4. The journey at a glance

What this section shows: the journey one person takes, from first hearing about AI risk to helping reduce it, and how that adds up to our goal. It reads top to bottom. A line marks where our work stops and other organisations take over.

Who the person is: someone from our audience (Section 3). By the end of our part they choose a route: join an organisation, start something, or campaign. The general public and people who are already convinced are on the map, but only our audience gets our time and money.

Part 1 — Seeing the problem and seeing their part (our work)

Eight gates. Each is set out in full in Section 5.

  1. Aware of today's AI harms, such as job losses, misinformation and bias.
  2. Aware AI could cause permanent harm, through power ending up in very few hands, people slowly losing their say (and the habit of thinking for themselves), or AI systems acting in ways humans can't stop.
  3. Believe the risk is serious enough to be worth real effort.
  4. It matters to them, because they care or because it affects them.
  5. Believe the problem can be tackled: people working together can change the outcome, so it isn't hopeless.
  6. See how they and their skills could help.
  7. Want to act: wanting to outweighs what it would cost them.
  8. Take a first step into one of the three routes.

A gate is a point where people move on or drop off. People can start part-way (we assume most of our audience already knows today's harms, to test), cross several gates at once, or skip some, and that's fine. What runs across all the gates is in Section 6.

Where our work stops. Our impact is the last move above: the person takes a first step into someone else's programme, organisation or campaign. Everything below, we point people towards, but others run it.

Part 2 — After their first step (other organisations' work)

1. Build context and skills, as much as they need: courses, fellowships and programmes, career advice, networking, research or writing. Showing you understand the field is often the price of entry.

2. Take on a role. This is where the routes split:

  • Joining → a safety organisation grows and delivers more
  • Starting something → more, and more varied, attempts to make AI safe
  • Campaigning → the public presses elected officials → governments act

3. The impact: safety organisations grow and deliver more, new ones start, and public pressure builds. So the field produces more and better solutions, and these get taken up by AI companies, governments and society. Together these lower the chance of AI permanently damaging humanity's wellbeing or long-term future. That is our goal (Section 2). We focus on lowering the chance of harm. Where these solutions also limit damage, that counts too. Who helps at each step is in Gate 8.

We're one contributor among many. Safety researchers, AI companies, governments, regulators and international bodies all work towards the same goal, and they do the work that actually reduces the risk.

What the journey is based on

The gates follow a pattern found in research on how people move from hearing about a risk to acting on it, mostly from climate change communication [12][13]. People become aware, then concerned, then come to believe action can work, then act. A consistent finding is that people usually stall not because they don't know or don't care, but because they don't believe their action will make a difference. So believing action can work matters most. We drew this together from that research; it isn't a single published model. It still has to be tested with our audience.

Gates 6–8 are a career change, so for those we also draw on research on how people choose careers: people choose a career path when they believe they can do the work, expect it to be worth it, and have support around them rather than barriers [14]. (The research is known as Social Cognitive Career Theory.)

The biggest assumption under every gate is that the main barrier is on the person's side (interest, or basic knowledge of the field), not organisations' hiring. If it's hiring, Gates 6–8 achieve little. This is Bet 2 (Section 8.2).

5. The eight gates

In short: one part per gate: what changes in the person, what we do, who else helps, why we think it works, how sure we are, what could go wrong, and how we'll know. Things that apply to several gates are in Section 6.

Gate 1: They're aware of the harms AI is causing today

A light gate: most people cross it without us.

What changes in them: they're aware of harms AI is already causing, such as job losses, misinformation and bias.

What we do: make, collect and explain content about today's harms that people can find and trust. Members of our community share it and invite others in.

Who else does this (most of it): their workplace (AI rollouts, training, restructures); being hit directly (a job threat, a deepfake, a data centre nearby); the news; other online content; using AI tools themselves; friends and colleagues. Working against us: AI hype, and "it's just a tool" framing.

Our real job here: to be found and trusted, so later gates can build on that trust.

Why we think it works: most people already lean worried: 51% of US adults are more concerned than excited about AI [15]. That we can become a source they trust is reasoned, not yet sourced.

What has to be true: most of our audience already knows about today's harms (to test). This gate also depends on two things that run across the whole plan: being found (big bet 3a) and being credible (Section 8).

How sure: medium that they cross it (to test). Low–medium that we're the ones who help.

What could go wrong:

  • Main risk: we're not found. Our content gets lost among everything else about AI, so we never earn the trust later gates need.
  • They learn about it elsewhere and never come to us.
  • We come across as alarmist.
  • We confirm their worries but give no clear next step towards the bigger risks (Gate 2).

How we'll know: the share of new starters (people who sign up with us) who already name a harm AI is causing today, and the share who come back within a month. If most don't already know today's harms, we start earlier than planned. Warning signs: few new arrivals are from our audience; many visit once and never return.

Gate 2: They're aware AI could cause permanent harm

What changes in them: they're aware that AI could cause permanent harm through three risks:

  • Power concentration: AI puts power in very few hands.
  • Gradual disempowerment: people slowly lose their say over the economy and society, and the habit of thinking and deciding for themselves as they lean on AI [16].
  • Loss of control: AI systems act in ways humans can't stop.

What we do: start with harms they already know, then show how each could grow into a permanent risk, starting with whichever risk is closest to their world. Where needed, we first explain enough about how AI works to make sense of the risks. We explain each risk simply, without jargon (Section 6). People discuss the bigger risks with others like them in our community, so the idea gets tested rather than just received.

Who else does this: leading AI scientists warning publicly (such as Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio, and the 2023 public statement on AI extinction risk signed by hundreds of AI experts); people seeing how fast AI is improving and imagining where it's heading; other explainers, books and journalism (such as AI 2027, a widely read scenario of how advanced AI could unfold); a serious AI incident; their peers or their own field treating it as real; and people we've helped passing the case on. Films and fiction cut both ways: they raise attention but make it sound like fiction. Working against us: sceptical experts calling it overblown, which gives people permission to dismiss it, and the "sci-fi" or "hype" label.

Our real job here: the world pushes both ways at this gate, so our work carries the most load here, and it has to be accessible, engaging and compelling.

Why we think it works: talking about the big risks doesn't reduce concern about today's harms; it adds to it [17]. In one small poll, arguments about people's own tools getting out of hand moved people most, and "experts say so" moved them least [18]. All three risks are serious published research [19][20]. Two message tests agree that talking about extinction moves people least. They disagree on what moves people most: one five-country test found risks to jobs and children did [52]; a larger trial found jobs did poorly, while a concrete route to disaster (AI in warfare) raised concern about extinction more than talking about extinction did [53]. So we start from concrete harms and show the route, rather than leading with extinction.

What has to be true: the link from today's harms to these bigger risks feels credible, not like science fiction.

How sure: medium.

What could go wrong:

  • Main risk: it's dismissed as science fiction. The permanent risks sound like a different, made-up kind of claim.
  • Sceptical experts give them permission to dismiss it.
  • They have no room for another worry.
  • "Who are you to tell me this?"

How we'll know: the share who, after our content, can name one of the three permanent risks and how a harm they know could grow into it. Warning sign: the share who say it sounds like science fiction.

Gate 3: They believe the risk is serious enough to be worth real effort

A deep gate: our work matters most here.

What changes in them: they understand how AI could actually cause permanent harm, and find it convincing.

What we do: explain how it could actually happen, step by step, with the evidence, so they can judge the logic for themselves rather than having to trust us. We answer sceptics' points honestly. People work through the arguments with others like them in our community, so the belief is reasoned and lasts.

Who else does this: expert surveys giving real odds to extreme outcomes; in-depth books, podcasts and long-form explainers; introductory courses (such as BlueDot Impact's free AI safety courses); their own reading; and people like them who were sceptical and changed their mind. Working against us: well-argued sceptic content, belief fading over time, and the news moving on.

Our real job here: others offer good explanations, but mostly in technical or policy terms. Our edge is explaining it to experienced non-technical people in their terms. Community makes belief last, because people keep talking it through with others like them; nothing else at this gate does that.

Why we think it works: in one small poll, explaining how moved people more than "experts say so", and counter-arguments pulled them back [18]. Stories and explanations shift beliefs a little each time (small but reliable effects) [21]. Many AI researchers give real odds to extreme outcomes [22]. Against: media giving sceptics equal airtime leaves people unsure what experts think [23].

What has to be true: the belief lasts long enough to act on. That runs across all gates (Section 8, row 4).

How sure: medium that the logic holds; low that it persuades.

What could go wrong:

  • Main risk (two parts): (a) it doesn't convince them: sceptical experts and counter-arguments pull them back [18][23]. (b) it doesn't last: the belief fades before they act.
  • It's too complex and they get lost.
  • They don't want to believe it, or already dismissed it.

How we'll know: the net change in how likely they rate the risk from start to end ("net" = people who moved up minus people who moved down), and the share still at that level three months later. Warning signs: the share who end up less convinced, and the share who have dropped back at three months.

Gate 4: It matters to them

What changes in them: it matters to them personally, because they care or because it affects them.

What we do: link it to what they already care about: the people they love, the future, their own values and sense of purpose. And show that it's unfair: who is deciding, who benefits and who bears the risk. We talk about it in their terms, not ours. For people leaning towards campaigning, fairness and who holds power are often the main link. In our community they care alongside others, and belonging keeps the concern alive. The approach is the same for everyone; only the examples change.

Who else does this: people they love raising it or being affected; a real event that makes it feel personal; peers or their field visibly caring, not just believing; films, art or a cultural moment; compelling voices and activists; and people like them showing why it matters to them. Working against us: doom fatigue and over-alarm, which make people tune out, and everyday life crowding it out.

Our real job here: the strongest outside pushes (loved ones, real events) can't be made and shouldn't be faked. Our job is to make the link, then move quickly to Gates 5 and 6 so concern doesn't turn into despair.

Why we think it works: arguments put in terms of the listener's own values persuade better [24]. People don't see losing the long-term future as especially bad unless prompted to think long-term [25]. Making a risk feel close to home is an unreliable tactic [26]. In one large trial, anger at a harm predicted wanting to act; anxiety didn't [53]. People join group action when they see an injustice [34].

What has to be true: understanding turns into concern, not despair.

How sure: low–medium.

What could go wrong:

  • Main risk: it stays abstract. They agree it's serious, but it doesn't touch anything they care about.
  • Doom fatigue.
  • Other priorities win.
  • The care fades.
  • They feel helpless and shut down.
  • Anger tips into sounding alarmist, which costs us credibility.

How we'll know: the share who rate it as mattering to them personally and can say why in their own words. Warning sign: the share who say they feel hopeless or want to switch off. Despair is the failure the research warns about.

Gate 5: They believe the problem can be tackled

A deep gate: the research says this is where people most often stall. They care, but think nothing will work.

What changes in them: they believe that people working together can change the outcome, so it isn't hopeless. (Whether they personally can help is Gate 6.)

What we do: show real, recent progress: safety methods that work, organisations making a difference, public pressure that has already changed AI decisions (such as local fights over data centres [27]), and people like them already doing the work. We're honest about what isn't solved. In our community they see others already working on it, so hope rests on real people, not claims.

Who else does this: AI safety organisations publishing results; news of new laws, commitments or wins; campaigns showing visible wins (such as the campaign groups PauseAI and ControlAI, and local data-centre campaigns); funders announcing money for the field; and people who made the move showing their work matters. Working against us: fatalism ("it's too late", "the AI companies will do what they want"); industry lobbying making public pressure look pointless; AI companies' safety claims that look like spin.

Our real job here: good news exists, but it's scattered and rarely aimed at our audience. Of all the gates, this is where gathering and framing others' progress counts most.

Why we think it works: most AI risks need a shared response, so what counts is believing that people acting together can succeed [56]. Warnings work best when paired with a way to act [28]. Without one, they may not change behaviour at all, and can make people avoid the subject or deny it [29][57]. Whether frightening warnings work at all is debated [58], so we don't rely on fear. Hope based on real progress and trusted organisations goes with action; hope based on denial doesn't [30]. On public pressure: believing governments will respond is the strongest single predictor of people taking public action [12], and public opinion does shape policy, most on visible issues and least when industry lobbies hard [11].

What has to be true: enough real progress exists, and showing it honestly moves people from "it matters" to "it can be tackled". Progress is real but patchy [31]. Public-pressure examples also need governments to respond when pressed (Section 8).

How sure: low–medium. This gate is new and not yet tested.

What could go wrong:

  • Main risk: it sounds like spin. Overclaiming loses their trust; underclaiming leaves them hopeless.
  • Good news is scattered or out of date.
  • AI companies' safety claims make all progress look like PR.
  • "They won't listen": industry lobbying makes public pressure look pointless.

How we'll know: the net change in agreeing "people working together can change how this turns out", and the share who can name a real example of progress. Warning signs: the share who call our examples spin, or agree "it's too late". Also: the net change in agreeing "public pressure changes what governments and AI companies do". Warning sign: the share who agree "they won't listen".

Gate 6: They see how they and their skills could help

A deep gate: this is the clearest gap we fill.

What changes in them: they can picture the work and believe they could do it. That's two separate things: knowing what's needed, and believing they can do it.

What we do:

  • For everyone: give a clear picture of how the field works, what the work looks like, and the basics people in the field are assumed to know.
  • Joining and starting routes: what the field needs by profession (operations, finance, people, marketing), including where new organisations are needed; role models from their own profession; and help to look back at their own past successes and see how those apply here. We don't coach founding: would-be founders are pointed to incubators at Gate 8.
  • Extra for the campaigning route: how organising skills apply to AI: who makes the decisions, which campaigns already exist, and where public pressure counts.
  • In our community they meet people who've made the move, which builds self-belief ("someone like me").

Who else does this: the field's own career guides (such as 80,000 Hours and BlueDot's introductions), which are real but lean towards technical and policy roles; career help for mid-career people moving into impact work (such as Successif and High Impact Professionals); practitioners talking about their work; someone in their own network already in the field; job boards and organisations' websites; events and conferences; incubators explaining who they look for; and people we helped, now working as role models. Working against us: the field reading as cliquey, elite or technical ("not for me"); existing guides sending everyone towards research or policy ("no place for someone like me"); and, for campaigning, AI framed as a tech problem rather than a fight about power ("not my issue").

Our real job here: others' guidance exists, but little of it is aimed at experienced non-technical people.

Why we think it works: experienced operations staff are among the hardest people to hire [5][6], the largest funder says it needs more founders [7], and guidance for these roles barely exists [8]. People believe in their own ability most from doing, then from seeing people like them succeed, then from being told [32][33]. For campaigning: people join group action when they feel something is unfair, believe the group can win, and identify with the cause [34]; strong organisations build people's skills, not just turnout [35].

What has to be true: the barrier is on their side, not organisations' hiring (Bet 2, Section 8.2).

How sure: medium.

What could go wrong:

  • Main risk: they don't believe they belong. They see the need but still doubt themselves.
  • We get the insider picture wrong.
  • We fix the wrong half (knowing what's needed vs believing they can do it).
  • It reads as a recruitment pitch.
  • They see they're needed but find no way in (big bet 2).
  • Campaigning route: "not my issue". AI looks like a tech problem, not a fight about power and fairness.

How we'll know:

  • Joining and starting routes: (a) the share who can name a specific role the field needs that matches their experience, or a gap where a new organisation is needed, scored as specific or vague; (b) the net change in "I'd be credible in that role". Warning signs: the share who say they're needed but see no way in (the early sign that hiring, not interest, is what blocks them), and the share whose self-belief falls.
  • Campaigning route: the share who can name who decides on AI and a campaign they could work through, and who describe AI as an issue of fairness or power. Warning sign: the share who still call it "a tech problem, not my issue".

Gate 7: They want to act

What changes in them: wanting to act outweighs what it would cost them (time, money, career risk).

What we do: remind them why it's worth it: it matters, it can work, and they fit. Make the first step feel small and doable: short steps, people who made the move and were glad, and an honest picture of the cost. Offer routes that fit their money and time. In our community others are making the same move at the same time, so it feels normal and less risky.

Who else does this: their own circumstances (savings, family, job security); their employer (sabbaticals, flexible work, or none); funded fellowships and paid programmes that lower the cost of trying; peers and family supporting or discouraging the move; and people who made the move. Working against us: the real cost of switching (money, time, career risk), and staying with what they know.

Our real job here: how able people feel is partly ours to change. How able they are (money, family) isn't.

Why we think it works: whether people intend to act depends on their attitude, what others around them do, and how able they feel. Whether they actually act also depends on how able they are [36]. People stick with what they have more than makes sense [37].

What has to be true: the full career move is worth the cost (pay, career risk, time) for enough people. This is on their side, not ours, and mostly plays out after our part ends.

How sure: low–medium.

What could go wrong:

  • Main risk (two parts): (a) it feels too costly: ours to change. (b) it is too costly: money, time, family. That's theirs.
  • They stay with what they know.
  • Family or peers discourage it.

How we'll know: the share who set a first step with a date in the next three months, and the share who rate that step doable for them. Warning sign: the share who say they can't, split into "feels too costly" and "is too costly", because we can change the first and not the second.

Gate 8: They take a first step

A deep gate, and our last. Where our work stops: after this step, other organisations take over.

What changes in them: they take a first step into one of the three routes.

What we do:

  • For everyone: a personal plan of small, dated steps. For most people the first step is building context (a course, a fellowship, or meeting people in the field), then a named route where one exists. In our community they get accountability partners, plus contacts and warm introductions into organisations, because hiring runs through personal contacts.
  • Joining route: a named route where one exists. Where none does, help to make their own way in: projects, writing, meeting people. We hand people to services that help experienced outsiders get hired, not straight into open applications. Those who already know the field can apply straight away.
  • Starting route: point them to a clear route (incubators, founder programmes, funders of new organisations) with a simple plan. A hand-off, not founder coaching.
  • Campaigning route: learn the AI policy basics and meet existing campaigns, then a campaign to join now, with a specific ask they can win.

Who else does this: courses and fellowships to build context (such as BlueDot's); open roles and job boards; services that help experienced outsiders get hired, such as referral and matching services (which pair candidates with organisations that are hiring), Generator Residency [38] and Lateral Workshop [10]; incubators (such as Catalyze, Halcyon and Constellation); grantmakers and funders of new organisations (such as Coefficient Giving, the field's largest funder, and Manifund); existing campaigns and advocacy groups (such as PauseAI and ControlAI); their own network; deadlines (application windows, programme start dates); and people who've taken the step already ("come with me"). Working against us: organisations that won't hire untested outsiders; very selective hiring and rejection; no funding for founders; nothing to join for campaigners; putting it off.

Our real job here: our plan and help are solid and can be improved, but the decisive thing, a real route in, is not ours. This is where what we depend on bites hardest (Section 8).

Why we think it works: simple "if this, then I'll do that" plans make people much more likely to follow through [39]. Without a plan, intentions often don't turn into action [40]. People who change careers succeed through small experiments and new contacts, trying things first and reflecting after [41]. Specific, winnable asks that people do together turn concern into action; vague ones don't [42]. Incubators and founder grants exist [43][7].

What has to be true: a clear, manageable route exists for someone like them, and the first step is affordable for enough of our audience. First steps are mostly cheap: free courses, short funded workshops, joining a campaign, and funded or paid programmes lower the cost further [10][38] (medium). Whether the route after us has room for them is in Section 8.

How sure: low–medium.

What could go wrong:

  • Main risk, by route:
    • Joining: no door. Organisations want people already proven in the field, and hiring runs through personal contacts [5].
    • Starting: not picked. Funding exists, but funders are short of people to assess new founders, so many wait or are turned down (Section 8).
    • Campaigning: nothing to join, or the issue becomes party-political.
  • The plan is ignored or put off.
  • "Make your own way in" becomes homework leading nowhere.
  • The hand-off is cold.

How we'll know: this is our ★ main metric (Section 9): the share of starters who take a commitment step within three months. We also count learning steps separately. What counts as each, by route:

  • Joining: commitment = a fellowship or programme application, or a matching service; learning = a course. Warning sign: the share whose first step was a cold job application, because sending under-prepared people into crowded hiring makes the field's bottleneck worse.
  • Starting: commitment = an incubator or founder programme, or an application for funding to start an organisation, project or programme; learning = a course. Warning sign: the share turned down for funding who then stopped.
  • Campaigning: commitment = joining a campaign's specific action; learning = the AI policy basics. Warning sign: the share who found nothing to join.

6. What runs across all the gates

In short: the things we do at every gate, not just one. Each gate's part (Section 5) says what they look like there.

Make it accessible. Plain language, no jargon, engaging, and in our audience's terms, at every gate. Others mostly explain AI risk in technical or policy terms; this is our main difference. Explaining how something could happen moves people more than "experts say so" [18].

Keep people moving. Losses multiply: if eight gates each keep 70% of people, only about 6% get through. Intentions and feelings fade with time [40], so every gate ends with one clear next step and no dead ends. Whether people stay motivated long enough is an assumption (Section 8, row 4).

Gates 4–6 close together. Once it matters to them, show quickly that it can be tackled and how they can help. A warning with no way forward is when warnings stop working [29][57]. Whether fear helps at all is debated [58], so we never rely on it.

Show the reasoning and let people reach their own conclusions (Gates 3 and 6 most). Show the logic and the evidence, including the strongest arguments against, and let them draw the conclusion. We're open that we hope they'll conclude AI needs to be made safe. It persuades better, and it protects our credibility as a small, unknown source.

Community at every gate. A real community that supports people through the whole journey, because people who move together keep moving. What it does at each gate is in that gate's part. People join group action when they identify with the cause and believe the group can win [34]; role models work when they feel similar and within reach [33][32]; what the people around you do shapes what you intend to do [36]; and career changers succeed through new contacts [41][5]. How sure: low–medium. The main risk, too few members taking part, is Section 8, row 6; it's measured in Section 9.2.

People bringing others in. People we help bring others in, so the journey feeds its own start. Someone newly convinced shares what they found (more reach at Gate 1). Someone already doing the work becomes a role model (self-belief at Gate 6). It compounds, if it happens at all. It isn't a separate activity: it's carried by our Community and Stories activities (Section 7). That it happens is not yet sourced (Section 8, row 6), and it's measured in Section 9.2.

8.2 The three big bets

In short: the three assumptions the whole theory depends on most. For each: the evidence for and against, how sure we are, and how we'll find out.

Bet 1 — More of our audience in the field means safer AI (rows 1a–1c)

  • The critic: "You aimed at the wrong people."
  • For: the field is tiny (about 1,100 people against about 19,000 building the most advanced AI, Section 1). Operations and management roles are a large share of job posts [49], and experienced operations staff are among the hardest to hire [5][6]. The largest funder says founders, not money, are the bottleneck [7]. Much of the danger is political, economic and social, and teams with mixed job backgrounds do slightly better on complex problems [50]. For campaigning, half of US adults are already more worried than excited [15], so the gap may be turning worry into pressure.
  • Against: the evidence is about "non-technical" people in general, not experienced people. We found no evidence that the field is short of organisers, so 1c is the weakest part, and one review found no solid evidence of too few founders [6]; whether new organisations add value is unproven, and they may duplicate each other (8.3). Some hard research problems aren't solved by adding people. Outsiders lack context.
  • How sure: medium for the joining route; low–medium for starting and campaigning.
  • How we'll find out: in the Bet 2 conversations, ask hiring managers whether senior operations hires made their organisations grow and deliver more. We'd change our mind if they didn't, or if funders said money, not people, was the limit again.

Bet 2 — What holds experienced professionals back is interest and basic knowledge, not mainly hiring (row 2)

  • The critic: "They're interested and capable enough. Organisations just don't hire them."
  • For: hiring managers say few applicants meet core requirements [5][6]. Most applicants rejected by one workshop for experienced professionals didn't understand the field's priorities [10]. When outsiders are routed in, many get hired: 12 of 27 from one residency within six weeks [38]. Large applicant numbers show volume, not readiness, and AI-written applications inflate volume everywhere (reasoned, not yet sourced).
  • Against: one recruiter argues organisations' hiring misses good people, with requirements leaning on field knowledge and jargon more than the job needs [51]. If that's the main block, our work achieves little for this group. Evidence on both sides is thin and mostly anecdotal.
  • How sure: low–medium. Not yet assessed for the campaigning route.
  • What we do about it: we specialise in the start (Section 3) and hand business professionals to the referral and matching services that help with hiring. The need grows as funding grows [7].
  • How we'll find out: about 5 conversations with hiring managers, and ask Lateral Workshop and referral services what happened to their applicants. We'd move to medium if hiring managers say applicants lack basic field knowledge, and to low if they say good applicants are ready but organisations don't take them.

Bet 3 — Our work actually moves people (rows 3a–3b)

  • The critic: "The logic is neat, but nobody actually crosses."
  • For: each gate has research behind it (Sections 4 and 5), and others in this field (80,000 Hours, BlueDot, Successif, High Impact Professionals) do move people in.
  • Against: that research is mostly from climate and health. No comparable programme publishes figures on how many people they move along. We have no channels into our audience yet, though partners already reach them (such as High Impact Professionals and Successif), so borrowing their reach is likely the fastest route in.
  • How sure: low overall. Finding us (3a): low. Moving them once they engage (3b): low–medium.
  • How we'll find out: for 3a, test one partner channel (a professional body or High Impact Professionals) and see if people sign up. For 3b, pilot about 20 people through Orientation and a First-step plan, measured by the main metric (Section 9). Ask BlueDot and High Impact Professionals for their figures as a benchmark.

9. How we'll know it's working

In short: what we count to see if it's working, and what we count to catch it going wrong. What we measure at each gate is in that gate's part (Section 5). This section holds the main metric and the measures that span gates.

Who we count: people from our audience (Section 3) who start with us, split by the route they choose (joining, starting, campaigning) once they choose one. Not the general public.

A "starter" is someone who signs up with us and answers our starting questions. Not a one-off visitor.

"Net" means people who moved up minus people who moved down.

Set later: how we collect each number, and the number to aim for, once we have a starting figure from the first people through.

The time windows are placeholders. "Three months" and "6–12 months" aren't from a source. We'll replace them with real timings once the first people are through. Because most people take a learning step first, we expect few commitment steps inside three months at the start.

9.1 ★ The main metric

Two kinds of first step. A learning step is one anyone can sign up for: a course or an event. A commitment step asks more of them: an application (to a job, fellowship, programme or funder), a matching service, joining a campaign's specific action, or starting a project of their own. Our main metric counts commitment steps only. Learning steps are counted separately, because for most people a course comes first and it's the easiest step to inflate.

What we count: the share of starters who take a commitment step within three months (what counts, by route, is in Gate 8). Reported by route, and by the gate they joined at.

Supporting number: the share who take a learning step within three months, and the share of those who go on to a commitment step. It never counts towards the main metric. Warning sign: many learning steps, few commitment steps.

Warning sign: the share whose step bounced: turned down, then stopped.

Why this one: it's the furthest point that's still ours, and the step that shows a real decision, not just interest. We split it by joining gate because people who join late are far more likely to take a step, so a change in who joins could move the number without our work getting better. Never read alone: always with the 6–12-month check in 9.2.

Whose: ours (the step). Others' (whether the door opens).

Stated willingness, not action. Authors' organisation campaigns on AI)
  53. Ostarek, Rogers, Kenward & Nadel (2026), Social Change Lab — Which AI harms and risks will mobilise the public to act? (randomised trial, 3,467 people, 11 harms; anger, not anxiety, predicted willingness to act; environmental harms mobilised most, jobs poorly; AI warfare raised extinction concern more than extinction messaging; mostly self-reported)
  54. Gilens & Page (2014), Perspectives on Politics — Testing theories of American politics (1,779 US policy issues; economic elites and business groups shaped outcomes, average citizens had little independent influence)
  55. Enns (2015), Perspectives on Politics — Relative policy support and coincidental representation (rebuttal to [54]: middle-income and affluent Americans usually rank policies alike, so the middle often gets what it wants)
  56. Bandura (2000), Current Directions in Psychological Science — Exercise of human agency through collective efficacy
  57. Witte & Allen (2000), Health Education & Behavior — A meta-analysis of fear appeals (strong fear with a weak way to act produced the most defensive reactions)
  58. Kok et al. (2018), Health Psychology Review — Ignoring theory and misinterpreting evidence: the false belief in fear appeals (sceptical critique of [28])
